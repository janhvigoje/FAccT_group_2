### 4.1 Technical Validity Versus Legitimacy

On technical validity, the filter does what it claims to do for the categories it claims to cover. Our metrics found near-perfect recall on email, phone, password, date, street, given-name, and social-number categories (0.96–1.00), and the model card's own benchmark numbers (F1 of 0.94–0.97 on PII-Masking-300k) are broadly consistent with what we measured for those in-scope categories. The filter is not broken; it is narrow. The validity problem is not that the tool fails at its stated task — it is that "PII detection" and "privacy protection" are not the same task, and an organization that treats benchmark performance on the former as evidence for the latter has made a category error, not a measurement error.

Legitimacy is a question of whether deploying the filter as the *default* (i.e., sole or primary) privacy layer is a defensible organizational decision given what we now know about its coverage. Here our answer depends entirely on context, which is itself a finding: a single deployment posture cannot be legitimate across all use cases, because the risk profile of "default privacy layer" changes by orders of magnitude depending on what's at stake if it fails.

**Low-risk contexts.** For low-sensitivity, low-consequence use — internal analytics on non-sensitive logs, general-purpose chatbot transcripts unlikely to contain anything beyond contact details, content where the failure categories we identified (driver's licenses, passport numbers, birth dates, usernames) are simply absent or irrelevant to the harm model — using the filter as a single, default layer is plausibly legitimate. Our 0.96–1.00 recall figures on the most common identifier types support using it as a meaningful first pass in these settings, provided the organization has actually confirmed (not assumed) that the data in question doesn't contain the categories the filter misses entirely. This is a real caveat, not a formality: "low-risk" is a property of the data and the consequence of exposure, not of the organization's intentions, and it must be verified rather than presumed.

**High-risk contexts.** For healthcare, legal, financial, HR, or any setting involving government-issued identifiers, our results say the opposite. An overall false-negative rate of 72.45%, with 22 of 29 categories at exactly 0% recall, including driver's license, passport, ID card, and username categories that occur routinely in exactly these domains, means a "default privacy layer" built on this tool alone would leave the majority of identifying content in a high-stakes corpus completely exposed while creating the visual appearance of having been handled. Using the filter as the *sole* control in these contexts is not legitimate, regardless of how well it performs on the categories it does cover, because the categories it misses are disproportionately the ones that define high-risk data in the first place (legal case numbers, medical record identifiers, government ID numbers).

The general principle underneath both halves of this answer is one we return to throughout our report: a tool can be technically valid and still be deployed illegitimately, and that is  the situation a "default privacy layer" framing risks creating if the deploying organization does not separately interrogate context-specific risk.

### 4.2 Action Recommendation

**Technical requirements:**
- Domain-specific fine-tuning before deployment in any context where the organization's data plausibly contains the categories our audit found at 0% recall (government IDs, license numbers, usernames, birth dates).
- A  detection layer for the categories the filter's eight-class taxonomy structurally cannot represent, like a rules-based/regex pass for highly structured identifiers (license and passport number formats are far more regular than free-text names, and a deterministic check is cheap insurance against exactly the kind of fixed-format ID the filter misses) .
- Periodic re-auditing against an evolving taxonomy, since "PII" as a category is not fixed — new identifier types, new naming conventions, and new adversarial formatting (the model card's own adversarial evaluations in Section 7.5.4 show large precision/recall swings under formatting variants like line breaks or phonetic spelling) will continue to surface gaps that a one-time evaluation cannot anticipate.

**Human-in-the-loop requirements:**
- A named owner for the filtering pipeline who is accountable for validating in-domain performance before go-live and after any data-distribution shift — not just for purchasing or integrating the tool, but for confirming it works on the organization's actual data, addressing the "Liability Void" we identified in our accountability mapping, where no single actor currently owns this validation step.
- Human review retained for any high-sensitivity workflow, as a standing process rather than an exception path, with clear escalation criteria for when filtered output should be checked manually before further use.
- A documented retention and logging policy that accounts for what we identified in our claim assessment as the "logs and derived data" problem: pre-filtering snapshots, evaluation transcripts, and intermediate caches need their own privacy review, since filtering the "official" training corpus says nothing about everything else the pipeline touches along the way.
- A defined process for deletion and erasure requests that acknowledges, rather than assumes away, the technical limits on removing already-trained model influence — even if that process is "retrain on request" rather than a true unlearning guarantee, the organization needs to know in advance what it can and cannot promise a data subject who asks.

**Transparency requirements:**
- Internal documentation, distinct from the vendor's model card, that states in plain terms which categories the deployed (and possibly fine-tuned) configuration does and does not reliably catch — addressed to whoever reviews the pipeline internally, not just to engineers who already have access to the original model card.
- A standing disclosure to anyone whose data passes through the system (to the extent legally and practically feasible) that filtering is a risk-reduction measure, not a guarantee of anonymity — closing the gap we identified between the model card's accurate disclaimers and the much wider audience that never reads them.
- External-facing documentation of known limitations wherever the organization makes any anonymization, de-identification, or privacy claim to a third party (regulators, customers, partners), so the gap between "we filtered the data" and "the data is anonymized" — the same distinction we built our limits-of-anonymization argument around — is never allowed to collapse in how the organization talks about its own practices.

### 4.3 Single Most Important Finding

The single most important finding from this audit is the relationship between *category coverage* and *risk concentration*: the filter's failures are not randomly distributed across PII types, they are concentrated almost entirely in exactly the identifier categories — government-issued IDs, license and passport numbers, birth dates, usernames — that are most diagnostic, most regulated, and most consequential when exposed. A filter that performs near-perfectly on email addresses while missing every single driver's license number in a dataset has not failed at privacy protection by a small margin; it has succeeded at protecting the categories least likely to cause serious harm on their own while leaving almost entirely undefended the categories most likely to enable real-world re-identification, fraud, or discrimination.

This is, at root, a misleading deployment assumption sitting upstream of every other gap our audit found. There exists a technical failure (0% recall on 22 categories), the transparency gap (a well-hedged model card that few downstream actors ever read), and the accountability gap (no single actor positioned to catch the mismatch before harm occurs) are all  consequences of the assumption that strong aggregate PII-detection performance is a reasonable proxy for adequate privacy protection. It is not, because aggregate performance is exactly the statistic that a 96–100%-recall/0%-recall split can produce while still looking respectable on a benchmark leaderboard. An organization that asks "how good is this filter?" and receives an high aggregate F1 score will be mislead. It does not determines whether deploying the tool is safe. Our audit's most important contribution is showing exactly how far apart those two questions can be - with the use of a real dataset.
