# 3.4 Limits of Anonymization

A fundamental misconception in AI data-preparation pipelines is the conflation of PII filtering with anonymization. Based entirely on our empirical metrics (Section 2.3) and qualitative downstream checks (Section 2.5), our audit demonstrates that these two concepts are entirely distinct. Anonymization is an absolute end-state where data is irrevocably stripped of its ability to identify a natural person. In contrast, PII filtering is merely a token-level text replacement operation. Relying exclusively on our dataset observations, we outline below why the OpenAI Privacy Filter (OPF) fails to achieve true anonymization.

### 1. Quasi-Identifiers and Residual Identifiers
A privacy filter cannot anonymize data if its taxonomy fundamentally ignores the building blocks of an individual's identity. As mathematically proven in our Section 2.3 Full Evaluation, the OPF provides zero protection for 22 out of the 29 observed PII categories in the dataset (yielding 0 True Positives). While it successfully masks explicit identifiers like emails (Recall: 1.0), it leaves behind massive amounts of quasi-identifiers—attributes that do not explicitly name a person but drastically narrow down their identity. 

Specifically, our dataset evaluation logged 920 unredacted Birth Dates (`BOD`), 843 Gender markers (`SEX`), 715 `POSTCODE`s, and 769 Job `TITLE`s. When the filter masks a direct name but leaves these quasi-identifiers behind, they become residual identifiers in the output text. As demonstrated in Section 2.5 (Example 3), these residual identifiers (such as the birth date "13/01/1942" paired with an IP address) survive perfectly intact into the downstream LLM summaries, meaning the core identifying attributes of the individual remain fully exposed.

### 2. Contextual Uniqueness and Linkage Attacks
Even if all standardized PII categories were detected, human language inherently carries contextual uniqueness. Although the OPF utilizes local transformer embeddings to detect supported entities, it fundamentally operates as a span-level Named Entity Recognition (NER) tool, not a semantic anonymizer. It detects a named entity but remains entirely blind to the privacy risks embedded in the remaining descriptive context. 

For instance, in Section 2.5 (Example 4), we observed that an unredacted IP address, paired with a specific title ("Pr") and its surrounding textual context, creates a highly unique linguistic fingerprint. A downstream adversary reading the LLM output can easily use these unredacted, unique contexts to launch linkage attacks. By cross-referencing these residual identifiers with external public records, an attacker can reliably re-identify the affected person. The catastrophic overall Full FNR of 72.45% documented in our metrics guarantees that more than enough context remains to execute these linkage attacks on the dataset.

### 3. The Danger of Over-Reliance
When organizations deploy tools like the OPF, the visual output creates a psychological trap that leads to over-reliance. In our qualitative check (Section 2.5), we noted that the downstream LLM outputs read fluently and look professionally redacted, featuring explicit tags like `[PRIVATE_PERSON]` or `[SOCIALNUMBER]`. 

This visual presence of redaction tags creates a false sense of security among deployers and internal review bodies. They see the tags and falsely assume the text is safe, failing to realize that 72.45% of the actual PII entities in the document were completely ignored. This over-reliance directly contributes to the "false reassurance" harm identified in Section 1.1, as organizations unknowingly pass heavily exposed data into LLM training pipelines with zero additional human oversight.

### 4. Masking a Text Span vs. Eliminating Privacy Risk
Ultimately, the most critical finding from our error analysis (Sections 2.4 and 2.5) is the vast distinction between syntactic masking and semantic safety. Masking a text span is merely an algorithmic string replacement. For instance, in Section 2.5 (Examples 2 and 5), we observed "truncated masking," where the filter masked the prefix of a driver's license or passport but left the trailing digits exposed (e.g., generating `[SOCIALNUMBER]892` and `[SOCIALNUMBER]469`). 

The filter successfully performed a "span masking" action on a subset of the tokens, but it completely failed to eliminate the privacy risk, as the downstream LLM faithfully reproduced the exposed trailing digits in its summary (*"ending in 892"*). This proves that eliminating privacy risk requires destroying the informational link to a human being, whereas the OPF merely acts as a fragile redaction tool that leaves the fundamental privacy threat intact.
