# 3.5 Why OpenAI Frames the Tool Cautiously

*Note: When we refer to sections, we mean the sections in the Model Card released by OpenAI, not the ones in our project. References to our own report's findings are written out by name (e.g. "our accountability mapping," "our fairness analysis") rather than by number.*

### What the Framing Actually Says

The model card is explicit, repeatedly, about what Privacy Filter is not. Section 3.2 states that it "should not be treated as an anonymization, compliance, or safety guarantee, a substitute for policy review in high-stakes deployments, a universal privacy oracle... or a legal determination system." Section 4.1 restates this almost verbatim: Privacy Filter is "a redaction and data minimization aid, not an anonymization, compliance, or a safety guarantee," and "[o]ver-reliance on the tool as a blanket anonymization claim would risk missing desired privacy objectives." The recommended posture (Section 4.5) is to use it as "one of multiple layers in a holistic end-to-end privacy-by-design approach," evaluated in-domain, fine-tuned where local policy diverges from the default taxonomy, with human review retained for high-sensitivity workflows.

This is not throwaway legal boilerplate bolted onto an otherwise unqualified capability claim. The caution is structurally load-bearing: it appears in the intended-uses section, the out-of-scope section, the bias/risk/limitations section, and the recommendations section, and it is backed by the model's own documented failure modes — under-detection of uncommon names and regional naming conventions, fragmented span boundaries in mixed-format text, missed novel credential formats, and degraded one-hop reasoning when an alias is defined far from where the sensitive value later appears. Notably, this last limitation is a near word-for-word admission of a re-identification-adjacent failure: the model card's own adversarial evaluation shows recall degrading as the distance between an alias definition and its later use increases (Section 7.5.3), which is structurally the same problem our project's scope statement describes as linguistic fingerprinting persisting after masking — context that survives even when explicit identifiers don't.

### Why This Framing Matters for Accountability

Our accountability mapping identified five actors and four forums, and named a "Liability Void" between the Model Provider and the Tool Deployer. The cautious framing is precisely the mechanism that creates and protects that void. Mitchell et al.'s (2019) original model card proposal argued that this kind of documentation exists to close transparency gaps between developers and downstream users so that performance characteristics, intended uses, and limitations are legible to whoever has to make a deployment decision. A disclaimer that PII filtering is not anonymization does that legitimate work: it tells a Tool Deployer, in writing, that further evaluation and layered controls are their responsibility before they can make any anonymization claim of their own.

But the same sentence does two things at once. It is simultaneously a transparency artifact and a liability-shifting instrument. Once OpenAI has stated, in the model card, that the tool is not a safety guarantee and that high-sensitivity use requires in-domain evaluation and human review, the documentation has done its job from a model-card-as-accountability-mechanism standpoint — and it has also pre-positioned responsibility for any downstream failure onto whichever actor skipped that recommended evaluation step. This is not a flaw unique to OpenAI; it is close to definitional of what a disclaimer is. The point for this audit is that "cautious framing" and "accountability" are not the same achievement, and a framing can satisfy the documentation half of accountability (the "define construct... report limitations" workflow from the FAccT-ML audit lecture) while doing comparatively little for the redress half — courts and regulators still need *evidence*, not just disclosure, to act on a harm, and the disclaimer itself produces none.

This matters concretely for our own metrics and our limits-of-anonymization argument. A 72.45% overall false-negative rate, with 22 of 29 categories at exactly zero recall, is consistent with the model card's own disclosure — nothing in our results contradicts what OpenAI said the tool would do. The model card defines only eight categories (Section 6.1); the dataset's 29-category taxonomy was never something the tool claimed to cover. So the harm in this case is not that OpenAI was dishonest about capability. It is that a true, well-hedged statement and a 0%-recall outcome on two-thirds of a realistic PII taxonomy are compatible facts that point to two different available reactions: "the documentation worked as a transparency mechanism" and "the documentation was not load-bearing enough to prevent the failure it disclosed." Both are correct, and which one a given actor emphasizes depends on where they sit in the accountability chain we mapped.

### Who Benefits From Cautious Framing

**The Model Provider benefits most directly.** If a Tool Deployer integrates Privacy Filter without in-domain evaluation, fine-tuning, or human review — the three specific mitigations Section 4.5 recommends — and a breach follows, OpenAI's position is that the failure mode was named in writing. This is a legitimate and arguably necessary form of self-protection (model providers cannot certify every downstream context), but it also means the cost of the documented uncertainty is borne by someone else by default, not by the provider, unless a deployer can show the disclosure itself was misleading or contradicted by marketing claims elsewhere.

**Tool Deployers with the resources to act on the caution also benefit.** An organization that reads Section 4 carefully, runs its own in-domain evaluation, fine-tunes on a representative sample, and retains human review for sensitive categories gets real protective value out of the framing — it tells them exactly where to look. This is an uneven benefit, though: it requires technical capacity, an internal review function, and the institutional slack to budget for a validation step before deployment — slack that the smaller or under-resourced deployers in our accountability map (compliance teams without ML expertise) are less likely to have.

### Who May Still Misunderstand the Tool

**Tool Deployers operating under time or resource pressure are the most exposed group**, precisely because the protective behaviors the model card recommends (in-domain evaluation, fine-tuning, human review for high-sensitivity workflows) are costly, and the disclaimer's existence does nothing to guarantee they are followed. Our "Performative Oversight" finding is the direct evidence for this: an Internal Review Body that sees the tool deployed with default settings, observes `[SOCIALNUMBER]`-style tags appearing in output, and never reads Section 4.2's point that the model "will only identify PII spans that match the trained label taxonomy" can reasonably conclude the deployment is compliant. The caution is present in the documentation; it is not present in the interface, and nothing about seeing a redaction tag in production prompts a reviewer back to the model card.

**Organizations using the filtered output (the third actor in our accountability map) are structurally unable to apply the caution even if they wanted to.** They typically do not see the model card at all — only a downstream LLM's summary of already-filtered text, as in our own black-box check. The caution in Section 3.2 and 4.1 is addressed to whoever integrates the model, not to whoever later consumes its output three steps downstream. By the time text reaches that actor, the hedge language has been filtered out along with everything else; what remains is a redacted-looking document with no embedded signal that 72% of its identifying content survived.

**Affected persons (the fifth actor) cannot access the caution under any circumstance.** They are not a deployer, do not see the model card, and do not see the redacted text at all in most pipelines — only the downstream consequences if re-identification occurs. The "Recourse Asymmetry" we already identified in our accountability mapping means that even a perfectly clear, perfectly honest disclaimer changes nothing about their position: they have no mechanism to demand evidence of which spans were or weren't filtered for their own data, regardless of how candidly OpenAI describes the tool's limitations to the actors who do receive the documentation.

### Connecting Back to the Project's Core Thesis

This section sits closest to our limits-of-anonymization argument that PII filtering and anonymization are categorically different operations, because OpenAI's own framing makes exactly that distinction explicit and treats it as the central caveat of the release. The difference between our finding and the model card's disclosure is not whether the gap exists — both agree it does — but who is positioned to act on that knowledge, and at what point in the actor chain we mapped the caution stops being legible. A well-written limitations section is necessary for accountability but not sufficient for it: it equips the actors closest to the documentation (providers, sophisticated deployers, auditors) while leaving the actors furthest from it (under-resourced deployers, downstream consumers, and affected persons) no better off than if the disclaimer had never been written at all.
